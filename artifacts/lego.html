After your verdict: the reviewer's checklist

Show what a careful reviewer, human or AI, should flag
  1. LEGO keeps 30%. The spreadsheet values 100% of the project. Its "significant minority" row is empty and never used.
  2. Who funds the factory, who gets the buy-back? The case says LEGO pays the INR 100 m invoice; it does not say the buy-back goes to LEGO alone. The NPV to LEGO depends on it.
  3. The price never moves. INR 120.50 for ten years against costs rising 2% a year takes unit margin from 13% to −4%. At 2% price growth the project NPV turns positive: this assumption decides the case.
  4. The discount rate is the wrong fight. A lower rate makes this NPV worse, because the large inflow comes last. The 4% premium still needs a source, and INR cash flows need an INR rate.
  5. "ROIC about 40%" is not in the numbers. ROIC peaks around 12% in 2026 and is negative from 2029.
  6. Sunk and financing costs stay out. The INR 2 m of R&D is spent either way; interest belongs in the discount rate, not the cash flows. The spreadsheet gets both right.
  7. Tax credits on losses. The model books a 30% credit on every loss. Inside a 70/30 venture, whose tax bill absorbs it?
  8. Market share. One limited-edition theme reaching 28% of all LEGO units sold in India by 2033 is an assumption to test, not a given.
  9. Mechanics. Tax (30%) and the discount rate (10%) are typed into formulas, not linked to input cells; reinvestment from 2025 references the depreciation row; the buy-back is booked in 2033 although the case dates it 2034.
