If it falls short: the economy

A shortfall is a demand shock: firms cut next year's capex and shareholders spend less. The share losses spread beyond the AI stocks; the part owned abroad does not cut US spending. First-round effects on US GDP, before multipliers. The Fed's response is shown below, on the cost of capital rather than on GDP.

The bust sets AI-heavy shares 78% down, as the Nasdaq fell from March 2000 to October 2002, and capex 55% down, what the payback rule cuts over two years. Chip makers lose twice: their valuation falls with the AI stocks and their revenue with the build.

Your setting

No shortfall

Revenue covers the capital. Spending continues and keeps adding to demand. The risk is the next vintage, not this one.

Your setting

Prices collapse, usage is real

It would pay if prices held. Builders and their lenders lose; the capacity gets used and cheap compute flows to every firm that uses it. Fibre after 2001.

Your setting

Usage falls short

It fails even at stable prices: too little use for the capital deployed. Investors and the economy both lose, and capacity sits idle. Lenders on single sites and GPU collateral take the first losses.

Rates in the bust

A shortfall pulls rates two ways. The Fed wants to cut and, with the loop on, the build-out's own pull on rates unwinds, so the risk-free rate falls. With inflation above target, the Fed delivers only what is left of the cut after the overshoot. Lenders to leveraged builders demand more. The same correction makes capital cheaper for the big four and dearer for whoever borrowed against a single site.

How the model works

Each slice of capex is turned into a level yearly charge that repays it over its life and pays the cost of capital: charge = capex × r / (1 − (1 + r)^−life), chips and buildings separately. The charge is scaled up by (1 + r)^(months to revenue ÷ 12), for the money spent before revenue starts. Revenue needed = charge ÷ margin. Fixed capacity (the default): revenue in year t = year-1 revenue × (contracted share + spot share × (1 − price fall)^(t−1)); contracts hold their price for the chips' life, and spot rents fall as newer chips arrive. Grows with usage (earlier versions): revenue in year t = year-1 revenue × ((1 + usage growth) × (1 − price fall))^(t−1), as if a fixed fleet could serve rising demand. The verdict is the present value of margin minus charge over those years. Buildings outlive the chips; the model charges them only their yearly share.

Street plan. Capex follows the analysts' path instead of the payback rule: the 2026 figure you set, your growth into 2027 (about 40%, between consensus and the banks' higher estimates), then your growth a year. The same loops apply, so prices, power, funding, inflation, the cost of capital and each vintage's payback respond to the spending even though the spending does not respond to them. Debt raised = capex × the debt share.

Rates and the build-out (optional). With the loop on, the cost of capital used = your setting + β × (capex − $730bn) ÷ US GDP, with β in points per 1% of GDP. Today's rates already price the planned $700bn. In the bust, the risk-free rate falls by the Fed cut plus, with the loop on, the unwinding of the cut capex's pull; the big four pay your rate minus that fall, leveraged builders pay it plus the spread widening. Both responses are estimates for the class to argue.

Five years forward. Each year's build is split between the big four and, with that loop on, leveraged builders. Each class's vintage is valued with the model above at its own cost of capital, that year's margin and its starting revenue (the year-1 revenue per dollar of capex, times the funding factor). In fixed-capacity mode the price factor applies to the spot share only; in grows-with-usage mode, to all revenue. On the payback rule each class grows capex by the plan after a vintage that covers its capital and cuts it by the shortfall (at most 60%) after one that does not. With moratoriums on, from 2027 the share you set of each year's build slips a year.

Rates: the big four's cost of capital = your setting + β × (capex − $730bn) ÷ US GDP − the Fed's move + the dollar's premium. Leveraged builders add their spread, which widens by your setting for every 10 points by which their last vintage fell short. Prices: capacity counts every live vintage plus the fleet already running (your share of the 2026 build, retiring over four years), with each year's dollar buying more compute at the rate prices fall; usage grows at your usage growth; when capacity runs ahead of usage, that year's prices are scaled by (usage ÷ capacity)^ε, up to 30% above trend when usage runs ahead. Power: power prices sit above trend by your setting for every 10% by which the AI fleet (every live vintage) exceeds the planned fleet ($730bn a year), and below it when the fleet is smaller; the margin falls by the power share of revenue × the rise, for every live vintage; consumer prices rise by 2.49% (electricity's weight in the basket) × that year's rise.

Funding: AI shares sit below their peak by σ × the share of capital the latest vintage fails to cover (at most 70%); the labs fall 1.5 times as far (at most 90%). The next year, the labs' new capital shrinks in proportion to their fall, reaching zero at your threshold, and the compute it would have bought goes unbought: each class loses revenue × its labs' share × the new-capital share × the shortfall in new capital. Contracts do not protect against this: a lab that cannot raise money cannot honour its contract.

Inflation, after the Policy Room's inflation block: it starts at the level set under "Rates in the bust"; each later year it is 2% + your persistence × last year's gap to 2% + 0.4 × last year's growth from capex and confidence, plus power and import prices. The 0.4 is my estimate. The Fed moves the following year: it cuts by φ × growth lost − λ × (inflation − 2%), never below zero, and raises rates by (1 + λ) × any rise in inflation above today's level, which markets already price. Growth from capex = the change in capex × the domestic share ÷ US GDP.

Confidence (switch): each year's new US-held share losses, on the Seven, the rest of the market and, if listed, the labs, cut spending by the economy panel's cents for households plus yours for firms. The dollar (switch): the dollar falls by your setting per 10 points of last year's AI share fall; consumer prices rise by 0.1 point and the cost of capital by 0.1 point for each 1% it falls. Both 0.1s are my estimates; April 2025 is the pattern, not a calibration.

The stack. Of the cloud layer's year-1 revenue, the labs' share is paid partly from the labs' revenue and partly from new capital, of which part comes from the labs' own suppliers. With the IPO window shut, the new-capital part disappears and the year-1 revenue used everywhere on the page falls by revenue × labs' share × new-capital share. Chip and server suppliers receive the chip share of capex when it ships, and keep their gross margin on it.

Share losses. Losses on the Seven, on the rest of the market (by your share of the AI fall) and, if listed, on the AI labs (1.5 times the AI fall, at most 90%) are added up; the part held abroad is removed before the spending effect is applied.

Not in the model: taxes, working capital, contracts shorter than the chips' life, rent earned after the chips' booked life, and the value of being in the race at all. Customers who pay this revenue must earn more than it from AI themselves.
